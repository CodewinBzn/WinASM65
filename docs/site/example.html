Example game: Star Catcher

A small NES game written for this documentation, code and graphics alike: a vertical shooter with a 3D stage between its levels, in about 3,400 lines of 6502 assembly, graphics drawn with the Graphics Designer, and a config.json that puts them together into a 24,592-byte ROM. Every example on these pages comes from it. This page shows how to build and play it, how the project is laid out, how the graphics are made, and walks through the code.

The game

A vertical shooter. The starry sky scrolls down past the ship, and its top half ripples like a heat haze: fly with the d-pad, fire with A or B. Meteors fall, saucers zigzag, hunters dive at the ship; on later levels divers stalk it and swoop, turrets aim at it, weavers swing and fire fans, giant meteors break in two, and squadrons come in V and in line. Shoot them, and catch the stars for bonus points. After about forty seconds a warning blinks and the level's boss comes down; it fights in three phases, fans of bullets, then aimed bursts, then a spiral and rings. Break it and a 3D stage follows: the ship, seen from behind, flies over a floor that runs at it, and the enemies grow as they come from the horizon. Then the next vertical level starts, harder. Three hits and a countdown asks whether to continue: START plays on from the start of the level reached, with three new lives and the score back at zero; at the end of the countdown the game is over. The code and the graphics are original (no tile, sound or routine is borrowed from another game) and may be reused freely.

Build and play

From the example_star_catcher folder, any of these builds the ROM, star_catcher.nes:

compile.bat                    # exports the graphics, deletes the previous outputs, then WinASM65 -l -c config.json
WinASM65 -l -c config.json     # the same build by hand; -l also writes src\*.lst

Run the command line from the project folder: on the command line the paths of config.json are relative to the current directory (see the config.json reference). Open the ROM in Mesen2, or any NES emulator: mapper 0, 16 KB of program, 8 KB of tiles. compile.bat first runs WinASM65.Designer export gfx when the designer is on the PATH; without it, the exported graphics committed in gfx/ are used.

In VS Code with the WinASM65 extension, open the folder and press F5: the extension finds config.json, builds the project and starts Mesen2 with the debugger attached. The committed config.json names the emulator in its Emulator section, mesen2 at an example path, C:\Emulators\Mesen2\Mesen.exe: point it at your Mesen2, or set the winasm65.emulatorPath setting (or emulatorPath in launch.json), which takes precedence. A breakpoint on line 259 of src/game.asm, the first instruction of NMI, stops at the start of every frame, as on the VS Code page.

The rules, as the code has them:

WeaverFalls one pixel a frame, swinging 24 pixels either way, and every 128 frames fires a fan of three straight down.23
Giant meteorFalls one pixel a frame; broken, it leaves two meteors that fly apart.45
BossComes down after the level's 40 × 64 frames (about 43 seconds) and moves from side to side. What comes down depends on the level's tier, (level + 1) / 2 up to 5: one of 16 picks of the tier's line of KindPick, so level 1 sends only the first four kinds and a few giant meteors, and the later kinds come more often from level 3 on. From the second tier, about one spawn in nine is a whole squadron instead: five saucers in a V, or four weavers in a line. At most ten objects and twelve bullets are on screen, and something appears every 40 frames on level 1, four frames sooner each level, down to 14. An enemy or a bullet that touches the ship costs a life; the ship then blinks for 64 frames, during which nothing hurts it. After the boss, LEVEL CLEAR shows for 150 frames and the 3D stage begins.

The 3D stage lasts 36 × 64 frames (about 38 seconds), with no boss. Rocks come straight at the ship (1 shot, 1 point), saucers sway across (2 shots, 3 points), fighters come slower and fire bullets that close on the ship (2 shots, 4 points), and pylons stand on the floor: shots do not break them, so they must be avoided. Then LEVEL CLEAR again, and the next vertical level; from level 9 on, the levels are all alike. The score has four decimal digits.

  • Input lists the three units. Each is assembled on its own into an object file beside its source: src/header.o, src/game.o, src/vectors.o. game.asm also writes src/game.symb, the JSON table of its global names.
  • Output puts five files end to end. header.o is already 16 bytes, so its "Size": "$10" only guards against a header that grows. game.o holds 10,537 bytes, assembled from .org $C000 (the three 1,024-byte screens included); "Size": "$3FFA" (always hexadecimal) pads it with zeros to 16,378 bytes, so that the six bytes of vectors.o fall on $FFFA–$FFFF, the last six bytes of the 16 KB bank. chr/tiles.chr and chr/floor.chr, 4 KB each, are taken as they are: pattern tables 0 and 1.
  • Emulator is used only to debug: the emulator the VS Code debugger and the monitor start, here Mesen2 at an example path to replace with your own. See Emulator bridges.
  • There is no Target section: the target is raw and the image is written exactly as concatenated, header included. The complete config.json example builds the same ROM with a Target section that writes the header instead.

Why vectors.asm depends on game.asm

        .org $FFFA
        .word NMI               ; once per frame, at the start of the vertical blank
        .word RESET             ; at power-on and reset
        .word IRQ               ; not used

NMI, RESET and IRQ are labels of game.asm. Units are assembled separately, so while vectors.asm is assembled it does not know them: it emits three placeholder words. Its "Dependencies": ["src/game.asm"] lets it read src/game.symb in the second phase of the build, where it finds "NMI":49262, "RESET":49152 and "IRQ":49352 and patches the words to 6E C0 00 C0 C8 C0. Without the dependency the build stops with Line 0 - File src/vectors.asm - Type Undefined symbol. The order of Input does not matter: every unit is assembled before any is resolved. See How units are resolved.

header.asm needs nothing from the others: it is the header, byte by byte.

        .org $0000
        .byte "NES", $1A
        .byte 1                 ; 1 x 16 KB of program ROM
        .byte 1                 ; 1 x 8 KB of character ROM (the tiles)
        .byte %00000000         ; mapper 0 (NROM), horizontal mirroring: $2000 and $2800
                                ; are two screens one above the other, for vertical scrolling
        .byte 0
        .dsb 8                  ; unused, zero

The PPU has room for two nametables, and the cartridge decides how the four addresses $2000, $2400, $2800 and $2C00 map onto them. With horizontal mirroring (bit 0 of byte 6 clear), $2000 and $2400 are the first, $2800 and $2C00 the second: two different screens stacked vertically, which is what a game that scrolls up or down needs.

The later graphics, the four mutants, the nebulae, the ship from behind, the 3D enemies in their three sizes and the whole 3D screen, are drawn by a script, tools/star_catcher_art.py: each picture is a few shapes (ellipses, polygons) shaded with an outline, a rim and a mid tone, cut into tiles and written into the assets, so that the designer edits them like the others. Running it again redraws them; the assets it writes are committed.

Tiles

The NES draws everything from 8×8 tiles stored in the cartridge's character ROM. Each pixel of a tile is a number from 0 to 3; the palette the tile is shown with turns that number into a colour, and 0 is transparent (or the background colour). A tile takes 16 bytes: eight bytes that hold bit 0 of each pixel, one byte per row with the leftmost pixel in bit 7, then eight bytes that hold bit 1. The Tile Set editor reads and writes chr/tiles.chr in that form.

Tile n is at offset n × 16. Pattern table 0, chr/tiles.chr, holds every tile of the vertical levels, the screens and all the sprites, for the background and the sprites alike (PPUCTRL is set to %10000000: NMI on, both from table 0). Pattern table 1, chr/floor.chr, holds the 187 tiles of the 3D stage's background; during that stage the NMI also sets bit 4 of PPUCTRL (PPUCTRL_BG1), so the background is drawn from table 1 while the sprites stay on table 0. The two make 8 KB, one CHR bank.

Letters at their ASCII codes

The font has 5×7 glyphs for the digits, the capital letters and !, - and :, each stored at the tile number equal to its ASCII code, with one empty column on the left and one empty row below. "0" is tile $30, "A" tile $41, and the space, $20, is a tile left empty. So a string in the source is already a row of tiles: .asciiz "CLEAR" emits 43 4C 45 41 52 00, and those are the tiles of C, L, E, A, R. Digits work the same way, so the score is drawn by adding '0' to each digit. Since the sprites use the same pattern table, the same tiles write the score, WARNING and LEVEL CLEAR as sprites.

The other tiles: $01–$02 the ship, $03–$04 the two frames of a twinkling star, $05 the meteor, $06–$07 two distant stars for the background, $08 the burst, $0A the heart of the game over screen, $60 the ship's shot, $61 a bomb, $62–$63 the saucer, $64 the hunter, $65 the heart drawn in sprites, $70–$7B the boss, 4 × 3 tiles, and from $80 to $F5 what the art script drew: the four mutants, the ship from behind, the 3D enemies and the nebulae. A tile that is another one flipped is stored once, and the metasprite rows flip it.

Palettes

The game loads 32 bytes into the PPU at $3F00: four palettes for the background, four for the sprites. Each entry is a colour number of the NES's fixed palette, and $0F (black) is the shared colour 0. They are edited in gfx/game.pal.json, where each palette has a name, and exported as:

        .byte $0F, $28, $38, $30 ; sprite 1: stars

        .byte $0F, $27, $37, $30 ; sprite 3: burst

The screens use background palette 0 everywhere: letters are drawn with colour 1 (white), the heart with colour 2 (red), the bright background star with colour 3 (yellow). The map uses it too, except for the nebulae: each cloud's metatiles exist twice, with palette 1 and with palette 2, so the same tiles make a violet cloud and a teal one. The 3D stage loads its own 32 colours, s3d_palettes: a sky, a red horizon, three blues for the floor, and the game's sprite palettes. Each sprite's palette is in the attribute byte of its metasprite rows: 0 for the ship, the score and the heart (white letters, a red heart in colour 3), 1 for the stars and the shot, 2 for the meteor, the hunter and the boss, 3 for the saucer, the bomb, the burst and the boss's hit colours.

The boss is twelve sprites, four by three; boss hit is the same twelve tiles with palette 3, shown while a shot has just touched it. The later mutants have two frames each, one after the other in the list, so the game shows the first plus bit 3 of frame_count. The 3D enemies come in three sizes, 8, 16 and 32 pixels, also in a row: the smallest plus the size number, 0 to 2. Screens and the sky

The title and game over screens are whole nametables, 960 tile bytes and 64 attribute bytes, drawn in the Screen editor and exported to title.nam and over.nam; title.screen.asm is just scr_title: .incbin "title.nam" and SCR_TITLE_SIZE = 1024. The sky is a map instead: 29 metatiles of 2×2 tiles (bg_metatile_tl, _tr, _bl, _br and bg_metatile_pal) and one byte per metatile in bg_play_map, 16 wide and 30 high (BG_PLAY_WIDTH, BG_PLAY_HEIGHT): two screens, one above the other, in 480 bytes instead of 2,048. Its last row of stars joins its first, so it can scroll in a loop. The 3D stage's background is a third screen, stage3d.screen.json, drawn with floor.chr: stars above, the horizon's glow at line 96, and below it the floor, bands that are thicker the nearer they are, coloured 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3… from near to far, crossed by seven lines that run to the vanishing point.

A tour of game.asm

The file starts with .include "nes.inc", then its constants (speeds, limits, the kinds of objects, the boss's tuning, screen positions), then its variables. The variables live in the zero page, where instructions are one byte shorter and one cycle faster. .memarea $0000 starts the reservation area, and each .res gives a name the next free address and moves on:

        .memarea $0000
frame_ready  .res 1             ; set by NMI, cleared by wait_frame
frame_count  .res 1
buttons      .res 1             ; held this frame
…
scroll_y     .res 1             ; the sky's first line on screen, 0-239 ...
scroll_nt    .res 1             ; ... of nametable $2000 (0) or $2800 (2)
…
obj_type     .res MAX_OBJECTS   ; 0 when the slot is free
obj_x        .res MAX_OBJECTS
obj_y        .res MAX_OBJECTS
obj_speed    .res MAX_OBJECTS   ; pixels down a frame
obj_dx       .res MAX_OBJECTS   ; pixels sideways (signed); bursts: frames left
obj_hp       .res MAX_OBJECTS   ; shots left to break it
…
shot_x       .res MAX_SHOTS
shot_y       .res MAX_SHOTS     ; 0 when the slot is free
…
        .memarea $0300
obj_sx       .res MAX_OBJECTS   ; the 3D stage: an object's middle on screen,
…
bomb_x       .res MAX_BOMBS     ; bullets, in 8.8 fixed point:
bomb_xf      .res MAX_BOMBS     ;   the pixel and its fraction
…

.res emits nothing: the names are only addresses, $00 to $AC in the zero page. The objects and shots are tables, one byte per slot in each, indexed by X. obj_speed is $74, which is why adc obj_speed,x assembles to the two-byte zero page,X form 75 74 (the hover card shows that form). What does not fit, the bullets' eight tables of twelve bytes and the objects' places on the 3D stage, goes to a second .memarea at $0300, after the stack ($0100) and the sprite table ($0200). stage3d.asm gives some of these tables a second name for its own use (obj_z = obj_base, b3_z = bomb_y…): the 3D stage and the vertical levels never run at once.

Start-up

RESET, at $C000, is where the console starts. It masks interrupts, turns decimal mode off (the NES's 6502 has none anyway), stops the APU frame interrupt, sets the stack, turns the picture and the NMI off, and then waits for the PPU to warm up, which takes two vertical blanks. Between the two waits it clears the 2 KB of RAM:

        lda #0
@clear: sta $0000,x
        sta $0100,x
        …
        sta $0700,x
        inx
        bne @clear

The wait is the macro wait_vblank from nes.inc. Its anonymous label - belongs to each expansion, so the macro can be used four times in the file:

.macro wait_vblank
-       bit PPUSTATUS
        bpl -
.endmacro

Then RESET hides every sprite, loads the palettes, enables the pulse 1 and noise channels (APU_STATUS = %00001001), seeds the random generator and draws the title screen.

Main loop, states and phases

The game is in one of four states. Each has an enter_ routine, which draws its screen once, and an update_ routine, called once per frame:

MainLoop:
        jsr wait_frame
        jsr read_joypad
        lda state
        cmp #STATE_PLAY
        beq @play
        cmp #STATE_OVER
        bcs @over               ; STATE_OVER or STATE_CONTINUE
        jsr update_title
        jmp MainLoop
@play:  jsr update_play
        jmp MainLoop
@over:  jsr update_over
        jmp MainLoop

bcs takes the two states from 2 up; update_over sends the continue screen on to update_continue. When the last life goes, enter_continue shows a band of the sky with CONTINUE, a number and PRESS START, written into the nametable with the picture off, the number in a sprite. The number counts down from 9, one a second; A or B moves it on at once. START gives three lives and a new score, and starts again the level the ship was on, from its beginning: a vertical one through leave_3d, the 3D stage through enter_3d, depending on cont_3d, which enter_continue saved from mode3d before screen_off cleared it. Past 0, enter_over.

@play and @over are local labels: they belong to MainLoop, and other routines reuse the same names (@loop appears in fourteen of them). Within play, a level goes through three phases, in phase: PHASE_WAVES, while enemies come; PHASE_BOSS, the warning then the boss; PHASE_CLEAR, after it broke. On the 3D stage, mode3d is set and update_3d runs instead, with only the first and the last phase. update_play runs the frame:

update_play:
        lda mode3d
        beq +
        jmp update_3d
+       jsr scroll_sky
        jsr move_ship
        jsr fire
        jsr move_shots
        lda phase
        bne +
        jsr spawn_objects       ; PHASE_WAVES
        jsr count_time
+       jsr move_objects
        jsr move_bombs
        lda phase
        cmp #PHASE_BOSS
        bne +
        jsr update_boss
+       lda phase
        cmp #PHASE_CLEAR
        bne +
        jsr update_clear
+       lda lives
        bne +
        jmp enter_over
+       jmp draw_sprites

Each + is the next anonymous label down: six of them in one routine, each reached from the line just above it.

NMI, the sprite DMA and the scroll

The PPU raises an NMI at the start of every vertical blank, 60 times a second: the only time video memory can be written while the picture is on. NMI, at $C06E, saves the registers, copies the 256-byte sprite table at OAM = $0200 to the PPU with one write to OAMDMA, and sets the scroll for the coming picture:

NMI:
        pha
        txa
        pha
        tya
        pha

        lda #0                  ; copy the sprite table to the PPU
        sta OAMADDR
        lda #>OAM
        sta OAMDMA

        lda mode3d              ; the 3D floor: its three colours, turned
        beq +
        …
+       bit PPUSTATUS           ; the scroll: x 0, y scroll_y of nametable scroll_nt
        lda #0
        sta PPUSCROLL
        lda scroll_y
        sta PPUSCROLL
        lda scroll_nt
        ora ctrl_bg
        ora #PPUCTRL_NMI
        sta PPUCTRL

        lda wave_on
        beq +
        jsr raster_wave
+       lda #1
        sta frame_ready

#>OAM is the high byte of $0200: the page the DMA copies. PPUSCROLL takes two writes, x then y, after bit PPUSTATUS has reset its latch; bit 1 of PPUCTRL picks the nametable the y counts from, and ctrl_bg adds bit 4 on the 3D stage, the background's pattern table. On that stage the NMI first writes three colours of the palette (the 3D stage); on the vertical levels it ends with the ripple (the rippling sky). Then it sets frame_ready and restores the registers. IRQ is the label of its last instruction, the rti, so the unused IRQ vector returns at once. The main loop waits for the NMI in wait_frame:

wait_frame:
        lda frame_ready
        beq @wait
        inc lag_frames
@wait:  lda frame_ready
        beq @wait
        lda #0
        sta frame_ready
        inc frame_count
        rts

frame_ready found set already means the NMI came while the last frame's work was still running: that frame was lost, and lag_frames counts it. The game never reads it; the test bots do, to check that the busiest moments (a squadron, twelve bullets and the ripple at once) still fit in a frame.

The scrolling sky

enter_play draws the map's two screens with the picture off: draw_sky with A = 0 writes rows 0–14 of the map into the nametable at $2000, with A = 1 rows 15–29 into $2800, each a row of metatiles as two rows of tiles, then the 64 attribute bytes from the metatiles' palettes. With horizontal mirroring the two nametables are 480 lines of sky, one above the other. Every frame, scroll_sky moves the picture's first line one line up those 480, so the stars move down:

scroll_sky:
        lda scroll_y
        bne +
        lda #240
        sta scroll_y
        lda scroll_nt
        eor #2
        sta scroll_nt
+       dec scroll_y
        rts

At the top of a nametable it jumps to line 239 of the other one: from the top of $2000 to the bottom of $2800, whose rows the PPU continues with those of $2000. The sky never needs redrawing, and the NMI hands the two variables to the PPU. Because the background moves, the score and the lives cannot be background tiles: draw_hud_sprites draws the four digits, a heart and the lives as sprites, from HUD_X = 16, HUD_Y = 8. The game over screen does not scroll, and gets the final score and lives in its tiles, from draw_hud, while the picture is still off.

The rippling sky

The top of the sky, 96 lines from line 8, waves from side to side like a heat haze: each line is shifted by a sine that moves on every frame. The NES has no register for that; it has the horizontal scroll, which the PPU reloads at the end of every line. Written in the middle of the picture, it moves the lines that follow. Many cartridges have a counter that interrupts the CPU at a given line, but this one, mapper 0, has none, so the NMI counts the time itself: once its vertical blank work is done, it waits a fixed number of cycles until line 8, then writes the scroll once a line, from the table Wave.

        .align 256
raster_wave:
        ldy #WAIT_COARSE        ; to the band's first line
@coarse:
        ldx #0
@inner: dex
        bne @inner
        dey
        bne @coarse
        ldx #WAIT_FINE
@fine:  dex
        bne @fine

        lda #WAVE_LINES/3
        sta wave_count
        ldx wave_phase
@lines: bit PPUSTATUS           ; a line: 114 cycles
        lda Wave,x
        sta PPUSCROLL
        inx
        ldy #19
@d1:    dey
        bne @d1
        nop
        nop
        …

A line lasts 113 2/3 CPU cycles, so the loop draws three lines a turn, of 114, 114 and 113 cycles, and stays in step. Only the first of the two writes to PPUSCROLL counts in the middle of a picture, the horizontal one; bit PPUSTATUS resets the latch, so each write is a first one. A branch that crosses a page costs one cycle more, so .align 256 starts the routine on a page, and Wave fills a page of its own: its index wraps by itself, and no read crosses a page either. WAIT_COARSE = 2 and WAIT_FINE = 23 were tuned in Mesen2, whose Lua scripts can log the line and the dot of every write to $2005: the first lands on line 7 at dot 332, in the horizontal blank, so it moves line 8 on.

The ripple keeps the NMI busy for almost half the frame. It is off on the title, game over and 3D screens (wave_on = 0), and the band starts near the top, so that the wait before it is short. With horizontal mirroring, the line shifted left shows on its right edge the left edge of the same nametable: the sky wraps round with no seam.

Reading the joypad

Writing 1 then 0 to JOY1 latches the eight buttons; eight reads then return them one by one in bit 0, A first. lsr a moves the bit into the carry and rol buttons into the variable, so A ends in bit 7 and Right in bit 0, the order of the BUTTON_ constants. The buttons that went down this frame are those held now and not before:

        ldx #8
@bit:   lda JOY1
        lsr a                   ; the button is in bit 0
        rol buttons
        dex
        bne @bit
        lda prev_buttons
        eor #$FF
        and buttons
        sta pressed

pressed is what START is tested against, so holding the button does not restart a game; buttons is what moves the ship, so holding a direction keeps it moving.

Firing

A new press of A or B fires at once; while either is held, fire_timer lets a shot go every FIRE_DELAY = 8 frames. The mask is an expression, BUTTON_A|BUTTON_B:

fire:
        lda fire_timer
        beq +
        dec fire_timer
+       lda pressed
        and #BUTTON_A|BUTTON_B
        bne @shoot
        lda buttons
        and #BUTTON_A|BUTTON_B
        beq @done
        lda fire_timer
        bne @done
@shoot: ldx #MAX_SHOTS-1
@find:  lda shot_y,x
        beq @found
        dex
        bpl @find
@done:  rts                     ; three shots in the air already

A shot starts at the ship's nose and climbs SHOT_SPEED = 5 pixels a frame; move_shots frees it near the top and calls shot_hits, which tests it against every enemy and then the boss. On the 3D stage the same routine finds the slot, and shoot3d sends the shot away from the ship instead.

Spawning with the LFSR

Random numbers come from an 8-bit Galois linear-feedback shift register: shift right, and if a 1 fell out, exclusive-or with $B8. It runs through all 255 non-zero values before repeating, and never reaches 0, which is why the seed must not be 0. On the title screen, START seeds it with frame_count forced odd, so how long the player waited decides the sky.

random:
        lda rng
        lsr a
        bcc +
        eor #$B8
+       sta rng
        rts

Every spawn_delay frames, spawn_objects (in mutants.asm) looks for a free slot (obj_type = 0) and fills it at the top, in a column that is a multiple of 8. Which kind comes depends on the level's tier, 1 to 5, which start_level sets to (level + 1) / 2: each tier has a line of 16 kinds in KindPick, and four random bits pick one of them:

        jsr random              ; the tier's line, one of 16 picks
        and #15
        sta tmp
        lda tier
        sec
        sbc #1
        asl a
        asl a
        asl a
        asl a
        ora tmp
        tay
        lda KindPick,y
        jmp spawn_kind

A kind that shows up more often in a line is drawn more often: tier 1 has three stars in its 16, tier 5 three divers. From tier 2 on, a random number under 28 (about one draw in nine) brings a squadron instead, five saucers in a V from the tables VeeX and VeeY, or four weavers in a line. spawn_kind then sets the new object up for its kind: its life from KindLife, a speed, a direction, a timer. start_level sets spawn_delay to FIRST_DELAY less four frames for each level after the first, but not under MIN_DELAY.

The enemies

move_objects adds each object's speed to its row and frees its slot past row 232. Then each kind has its own move, found in a table of addresses and called through an indirect jump:

        asl a
        tay
        lda MoveKind,y
        sta ptr
        lda MoveKind+1,y
        sta ptr+1
        jsr @call
        …
@call:  jmp (ptr)

MoveKind:
        .word move_nothing, move_nothing, move_meteor, move_saucer, move_hunter, move_nothing
        .word move_diver, move_turret, move_weaver, move_nothing

The 6502 has no indirect jsr, so jsr @call calls a jmp (ptr): the routine's rts comes back after the jsr. A saucer moves sideways by obj_dx (1 or $FF, which added is −1), turns back at the sides by negating it, and fires straight down when a random number is under 3: 1 or 2, since the generator never gives 0, so about once in 128 frames. A hunter steps one pixel towards the ship every other frame. A diver slides over the ship until its obj_timer runs out, then dives faster and faster. A turret fires at the ship every 70 frames. A weaver's x is the middle of its swing, obj_base, plus Swing, a sine of 64 entries indexed by its timer. A meteor moves sideways by obj_dx, which is 0 unless it is a piece of a giant one: split_rock makes two, one going left and one right. A burst, what is left of a broken enemy, is the same slot with KIND_BURST, its obj_dx counting down the frames until it disappears.

Bullets and aiming

The enemies' bullets, in bullets.asm, go in one of sixteen directions, 0 to the right, 4 down, 8 to the left, 12 up. A direction 22.5 degrees off the vertical moves about 0.41 pixel sideways for each pixel down, so whole pixels would not do: a bullet's position and velocity are in 8.8 fixed point, a pixel and 256ths of a pixel, in two tables each (bomb_x and bomb_xf, …). fire_bullet takes the direction in A, the start in new_x and new_y, and the speed in half pixels a frame in bullet_speed; the velocity is the direction's half pixel from DirXL, DirXH, DirYL, DirYH, added that many times. Moving a bullet is two 16-bit additions:

        lda bomb_yf,x
        clc
        adc bomb_vyl,x
        sta bomb_yf,x
        lda bomb_y,x
        adc bomb_vyh,x

The carry of the fractions' addition goes into the pixels'; a negative velocity is the two's complement of the pair, $FF in its high byte. aim finds the direction to the ship without dividing: it halves the two distances so that they fit signed bytes, and their signs give the quadrant. Then which is the larger gives the nearer axis, and how far the smaller one is from 0 gives the steps off it: under a fifth of the larger, none; under two thirds, one; else two, the diagonal. The patterns are made of fire_bullet calls: shoot_aimed, shoot_fan3 and shoot_fan5 around a direction, one step apart, and shoot_ring, eight bullets two steps apart.

Collisions

Every test is one routine, overlap, on two boxes in zero page: box a (box_ax, box_ay, box_aw, box_ah) and box b. Small routines fill them: ship_box (the ship's body, 12 × 7 inside its 16 × 8), object_box (8 × 8, 16 × 8 for a saucer, 16 × 16 for the later mutants), boss_box (32 × 20). Two boxes overlap when each starts before the other ends, four unsigned comparisons each made of a bcc and a beq:

overlap:
        lda box_bx
        clc
        adc box_bw
        cmp box_ax
        bcc @no
        beq @no
        lda box_ax
        clc
        adc box_aw
        cmp box_bx
        bcc @no
        beq @no
        …
        sec
        rts
@no:    clc
        rts

The answer is in the carry, so a caller writes jsr overlap then bcc. A shot that touches an enemy is gone and takes one of the enemy's obj_hp; at none left the enemy becomes a burst and scores its KindPoints, and a giant meteor leaves its two pieces. An object that touches the ship is a star caught (5 points, the catch sound), or an enemy that bursts and costs a life, unless the ship is still blinking: ship_hit sets hit_timer to 64, and while it runs nothing hurts the ship.

The boss

count_time counts the frames of waves in units of 64 (when the low six bits of frame_count are 0). At LEVEL_LENGTH = 40 units, start_boss stops the waves: phase becomes PHASE_BOSS, phase_timer counts the 128 frames of the warning, and the boss gets its life, BOSS_LIFE plus eight a level:

        lda level               ; BOSS_LIFE, and 8 more a level
        sec
        sbc #1
        asl a
        asl a
        asl a
        clc
        adc #BOSS_LIFE
        sta boss_hp
        lsr a                   ; its phases: below 5/8 of its life, then 5/16
        lsr a
        sta tmp
        lsr a
        lsr a
        clc
        adc tmp
        sta boss_t2
        asl a
        sta boss_t1

A quarter plus a sixteenth is 5/16, and twice that 5/8: two shifts and an addition instead of a division by three. Meanwhile draw_message blinks WARNING with bit 4 of the timer. Then update_boss brings the boss down to row 32, moves it from side to side between x = 16 and x = 208, and fires by its phase, comparing boss_hp with the two thresholds. In the first, a fan of five every BOSS_FIRE_DELAY = 48 frames. In the second it moves two pixels a frame, and every 64 frames boss_burst loads three shots that go 8 frames apart, aimed. In the third a spiral: every 8 frames one bullet, in boss_spin, which turns three directions each time, and a ring of eight every 128 frames. boss_fights answers in the carry whether the boss is on screen and can be hit. Each hit sets boss_flash for four frames, during which draw_boss uses SPR_MS_BOSS_HIT; in its last phase the boss glows with the same colours every other 16 frames. When boss_hp reaches 0, hit_boss scores 50, leaves five bursts where the boss was (BurstX, BurstY), plays the long crash and switches to PHASE_CLEAR. LEVEL CLEAR shows for 150 frames, on two lines of five sprites, since a line shows at most eight; then update_clear moves to the next level, up to LAST_LEVEL = 9, and enter_3d starts the 3D stage.

The 3D stage

stage3d.asm plays the stage between two vertical levels, seen from behind the ship. enter_3d loads the stage's screen and its 32 colours with the picture off, sets mode3d and ctrl_bg, and the frame goes to update_3d. After 36 × 64 frames, LEVEL CLEAR; then leave_3d redraws the sky, and the next vertical level starts.

Each enemy has a place in front of the ship: obj_x across, signed, 0 in the middle; obj_y down, 0 at the eye and 80 on the floor; and obj_z away, from 250 at the horizon to 16 at the ship. Each frame it comes two closer, a fighter one. project puts a point on the screen the way a camera does: what is twice as far is half as big, so the offset from the vanishing point is the coordinate times 2048 / z, read from the 256-byte table Scale instead of divided, then divided by 64:

        x' = 128 + x × Scale[z] / 64
        y' =  90 + y × Scale[z] / 64

scale_by does the multiplication, eight bits by eight, a shift and an addition per bit, and a result that does not fit a byte is off the screen. place keeps the object's point in obj_sx and obj_sy, and picks its picture's size from z: 32 pixels nearer than 45, 16 nearer than 90, else 8, the three metasprites that follow each other. An object whose picture would cross a side of the screen is not drawn, rather than wrapped round to the other side.

The shots start at z = 24, where Scale is 85, four thirds: there the ship's point on the screen, ship_wx and ship_wy, is three quarters of its distance from the vanishing point, A − A/4 with the sign kept by cmp #$80 and ror a. They fly away six a frame and hit what is near them in x, y and z. The enemies and the fighters' bullets hit the ship on the screen: once nearer than z = 44, their picture's box is tested against the ship's with overlap, like on the vertical levels. A fighter's bullet comes three closer a frame, and a unit across and down nearer to the ship.

The floor moves without a byte of the nametable changing. Its bands are coloured 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3… from near to far, and every FLOOR_STEP = 5 frames turn_floor moves floor_phase on by one; the NMI writes the floor palette's three colours from FloorColors at that offset:

FloorColors: .byte $01, $11, $21, $01, $11

Each band then takes the colour the band behind it had, and the bands seem to run at the ship. Since the bands are thicker near the ship, as the floor's perspective has them, they run faster there too. Racing games of the time drew their roads the same way.

The decimal score

The score is four bytes, one decimal digit each, most significant first. Adding a point is a carry that ripples from the last digit: a digit that reaches 10 becomes 0 and the one before it is incremented. Keeping one digit per byte makes drawing trivial (digit + '0') and needs neither decimal mode, which the NES's CPU lacks, nor a binary-to-decimal conversion. add_points adds A points by calling add_point A times.

add_point:
        ldy #3
@carry: lda score,y
        clc
        adc #1
        cmp #10
        bcc @store
        lda #0
        sta score,y
        dey
        bpl @carry
        rts                     ; 9999 + 1: back to 0000

Sound

Four effects, each a handful of register writes; the APU plays them on its own. A shot is a short falling note on pulse channel 1, catching a star a rising blip on the same channel, a hit a crash on the noise channel, and the boss breaking a longer, lower crash.

sound_shot:
        lda #%01010110          ; duty 25%, constant volume 6
        sta SQ1_VOL
        lda #%10010010          ; sweep down
        sta SQ1_SWEEP
        lda #$60
        sta SQ1_LO
        lda #%00101000          ; short length, period high bits 0
        sta SQ1_HI
        rts

Drawing metasprites

Every frame of play, draw_sprites rebuilds the sprite table at OAM, which the next NMI copies to the PPU: the score, the ship, the shots, the bombs, the boss, the message, then the objects. oam_index is the next free entry; ms_x and ms_y are where the origin goes. draw_metasprite takes the metasprite's number in A, finds its rows through the two address tables, and adds the origin to each offset:

draw_metasprite:
        tax
        lda spr_metasprites_lo,x
        sta ptr
        lda spr_metasprites_hi,x
        sta ptr+1
        ldy #0
        ldx oam_index
@next:  lda (ptr),y             ; dy, or the end
        cmp #$80
        beq @done
        clc
        adc ms_y
        sta OAM,x
        …

An object's metasprite comes from the table KindSprite, indexed by its kind; the later mutants add bit 3 of frame_count, their second frame. A star plays the twinkle instead: anim_frame takes the animation in ptr and a time in A (frame_count), subtracts the durations row by row until the time falls inside one, and returns that row's metasprite; past the $FF it starts again, so the animation loops. The ship is SPR_MS_SHIP, hidden every other four frames while hit_timer runs. Single sprites, the digits and the letters of the messages, go through put_sprite. hide_rest then moves every unused entry off screen (Y = $FF).

The hardware shows at most eight sprites on a line and drops the ones after them in the table. So draw_objects walks the ten object slots backwards on even frames and forwards on odd ones: where too many share a line, a different one is dropped each frame, and they flicker instead of vanishing. draw3d does the same on the 3D stage.

draw_objects:
        lda #MAX_OBJECTS-1
        sta draw_count
@loop:  ldx draw_count
        lda frame_count
        lsr a
        bcc +
        lda #MAX_OBJECTS-1      ; odd frames: the other way round
        sec
        sbc draw_count
        tax
+       stx obj_index

The table has room for 64 sprites; a squadron, twelve bullets and the boss can ask for more. draw_metasprite stops when its index wraps round past the end of the table, and what did not fit is not drawn that frame.

Screens with the picture off

enter_title, enter_play, enter_over, enter_3d and leave_3d redraw the whole background, far more than a vertical blank allows, so each starts with screen_off, which disables the NMI and the rendering, puts the scroll back at the top of $2000, turns the ripple and the 3D stage's settings off and loads the game's palettes again; with the picture off, video memory can be written at any time. screen_on turns both back on at the next vertical blank.

The title and game over screens come from the designer whole. load_nametable copies the 1,024 bytes at ptr to the nametable, four pages of 256 through lda (ptr),y:

        lda #<scr_title         ; the screen made in the designer
        sta ptr
        lda #>scr_title
        sta ptr+1
        jsr load_nametable

draw_sky finds where each screen of the map starts, and which nametable it goes to, in three small tables built with <, > and parentheses:

SkyLow:       .byte <bg_play_map, <(bg_play_map+BG_PLAY_WIDTH*SKY_ROWS)
SkyHigh:      .byte >bg_play_map, >(bg_play_map+BG_PLAY_WIDTH*SKY_ROWS)
SkyNametable: .byte >NT, >NT_LOW

The parentheses matter: > binds tighter than +, so without them >bg_play_map+240 would be the high byte of bg_play_map plus 240.
